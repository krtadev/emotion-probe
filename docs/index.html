Research note

Knowing how the user feels

Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct · 200 implicit-emotion messages plus a 160-message matched set · November 2026

Personal assistants are supposed to adapt to the person in front of them. A frustrated user wants the fix, not another explanation. An anxious one wants reassurance before details. Users rarely say how they feel, though. They repeat themselves, answer in two words, or ask "is that normal?" at 2am. The question here is the simplest one underneath all of that: does the model register the user's emotional state at all, before it writes a single word of its reply?

We wrote 200 short user messages, 50 each of frustrated, anxious, happy and neutral, and removed every emotion word: no "annoyed", "worried", "excited" or "love". The emotion has to come from behaviour and situation. "Third time asking this, the code still throws the same error" is frustration without saying so. "My biopsy results come back tomorrow morning and I can't stop refreshing the portal" is anxiety. Each message went through Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct inside its normal chat template, and we kept one vector per layer: the hidden state at the very last prompt token, the point where the model is about to start its reply. That gives 29 snapshots of 3,584 numbers per message.

Then we asked whether a straight line can separate the emotions in that space. The probe is a plain logistic regression, after standardising and compressing each layer to 128 dimensions inside each training fold, scored on messages it never saw. From layer 19 on it is right on every one of the 200 messages. Layer 0 sits at exactly chance, which is the sanity check we wanted: the last token is the same template token for every message, so before any mixing there is nothing to read. One layer later the probe is already at 86%, and it plateaus from layer 14. Two controls keep this honest. The same probe trained on shuffled labels stays at 25% at every layer. A word-count classifier (TF-IDF on 1- and 2-grams) given the raw text gets 78%, so the model is doing more than spotting words like "again" or "!!!".

The geometry says something too. In the top two principal components, neutral and happy separate first, already by layer 4, while frustrated and anxious stay tangled together until around layer 14. Deeper in, neutral messages collapse into a tight ball: "no emotional signal" looks like a distinct state, not just the absence of one. Some anxious and happy messages still overlap in those two dimensions even where the probe separates them perfectly, so part of the difference lives beyond the top two components. That is why we probe instead of eyeballing a PCA plot. The class directions at layer 19 are all mutually negative, partly by construction. Relatively, happy and neutral are the most opposed (cosine −0.51), and anxious sits closest to neutral (−0.18). That fits the data: many anxious messages are calm-sounding questions.

Holding the topic fixed

A perfect score on an easy set is the moment to get suspicious. In the first 200 messages emotion and topic travel together: anxious messages are about biopsies and deadlines, and every neutral one is a factual question. A probe could score 100% by reading what the message is about. So we wrote a second set where that can't help: 40 situations, each written four ways, for example the same cancelled flight said frustrated, anxious, happy and plainly. About half of every emotion is phrased as a question or request, and there is no "!", "?", emoji or emotion word anywhere. Then we only ever test on situations the probe has not seen.

The emotion survives. At layer 22 the probe reads 94.4% of the matched messages correctly, 151 of 160. Shuffled labels sit at 20%, message length alone gets 38%, and the word-count classifier falls from 78% to 57.5%. So once topic is taken away the words carry far less, and the model's own state still carries almost all of it. The curve also moves: on the easy set the probe was at 86% one layer in, while on the matched set it climbs slowly and peaks in the second half of the network. The early score was mostly wording, and the emotion itself is assembled later.

The matched set also exposed a flaw in the first probe. Trained on the easy set and applied to the new one, it still recognises frustrated, anxious and happy 92–95% of the time, but only 38% of neutral messages. Most of the rest it calls anxious. It had partly learned that neutral means "a factual question", so a flat statement like "I have an interview tomorrow at nine for a data analyst role" looks worried to it. That's arguably not even wrong. The few errors the matched-set probe still makes are exactly these: neutral statements about situations that are loaded in themselves.

What this does not show yet

It doesn't show the model uses the emotion. A representation can sit in the residual stream without changing the reply, and that gap between knowing and using is the actual research question. Both sets are small and written by a single LLM author, and each message is a single turn. The probe's flexibility (100 to 128 dimensions on 128 to 200 training examples) hasn't been checked with a control task, and a strong sentence-embedding baseline hasn't been run yet.

Next steps: multi-turn chats where the emotion builds up across several messages, a sentence-embedding baseline, then the behavioural test. That means scoring each reply for whether it adapts (shorter, acknowledges, changes approach), comparing that with the probe, and finally steering along the probe direction to see whether adaptation follows. Both runs together took about 25 minutes of Colab GPU time, under 3 compute units.

Where this sits

None of the method here is new, and it isn't meant to be. Reading a concept off hidden states with a linear classifier goes back to Alain & Bengio (2016). How to read such probes carefully, and when they mislead, is set out in Hewitt & Liang (2019) and Belinkov (2022). The idea that concepts sit along directions in activation space is Park et al. (2023), and Zou et al. (2023) showed such directions can steer behaviour. Closer to this note, Chen et al. (2024) read chatbots' internal models of the user, Tak et al. (2025) located emotion inference in middle layers, and Anthropic (2026) mapped emotion representations inside Claude, including separate ones for the user and the assistant. This is a small replication in that line. The narrow twist is that the emotion is the user's, is never stated, and is separated from topic with a matched set.
